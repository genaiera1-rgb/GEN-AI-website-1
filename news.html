First Comprehensive University AI Policy [#SouthAfrica]

North-West University (NWU) has made history as the first South African institution to adopt an official, comprehensive AI policy approved by the University Council. Spearheaded by Prof. Anné Verhoef and the NWU AI Hub, the policy creates a centralized governance model that locates AI oversight within the IT department while utilizing a multi-stakeholder AI Steering Committee.

The framework proactively addresses rapid technological shifts by remaining flexible, covering teaching, research, and postgraduate supervision. Crucially, it moves beyond technicalities to include "Human-Centric" ethics and environmental sustainability, specifically targeting the ecological impact of AI's energy and water consumption.

MoltBot in Education: What It Does, How to Use It, and Key Cautions

MoltBot (OpenClaw) is a locally run AI assistant designed to automate everyday digital tasks while allowing users to retain control over their data. It operates directly on a user's computer and can integrate with email, calendars, files, and messaging platforms such as WhatsApp, Telegram, Discord, and Slack.

What MoltBot Does and How Educators Can Use It

MoltBot helps educators manage emails, organize teaching and research files, schedule meetings, send reminders, and automate routine administrative workflows. It works through simple natural-language instructions, allowing educators to delegate repetitive tasks while focusing on teaching, research, and student support.

Cautions and Potential Dangers

Despite its benefits, MoltBot requires careful use. Because it operates locally and can access files, messages, and accounts, improper configuration may expose sensitive student or institutional data. Educators should avoid connecting MoltBot to confidential systems without fully understanding permission settings.

In addition, automated actions can make mistakes if instructions are unclear, leading to unintended file changes or message distribution. There is also a risk of over-reliance on automation, which may reduce professional judgment in academic decision-making.

Finally, institutions should consider policy alignment, ethical use, and data protection regulations before adopting such tools.

Conclusion

Used responsibly, MoltBot can support educational work, but it should complement — not replace — human oversight and professional accountability.

The Importance of a Google Scholar Profile for Educators and Researchers

In today’s scholarly environment, visibility and impact are as important as publication itself. Academic work achieves its full value only when it is discoverable, accessible, and measurable. One of the most effective tools for achieving this is a Google Scholar profile.

A Google Scholar profile is a personal academic page that automatically tracks an author’s publications, citations, and research impact. It serves as a digital academic identity that allows colleagues, students, institutions, and global researchers to easily find and assess one’s scholarly contributions.

Why Every Academic or Researcher Needs a Google Scholar Profile

First, a Google Scholar profile enhances research visibility. Publications indexed on Google Scholar become easily searchable worldwide, increasing readership and citations.

Second, it supports academic credibility and professional recognition. Funding agencies, promotion committees, journal editors, and collaborators increasingly rely on citation metrics from Google Scholar when assessing research output.

Third, it enables accurate tracking of research impact. Instead of manually counting citations, Google Scholar automatically updates citation data as new works reference your publications.

Finally, it strengthens institutional ranking and research reputation. When academic staff maintain active and accurate profiles, the collective research impact of the institution becomes more visible globally.

Understanding Key Research Metrics

A major advantage of Google Scholar is its use of simple but powerful citation indicators. Among the most important are the h-index and the i10-index.

The h-index Explained

The h-index measures both productivity and citation impact. An academic has an h-index of h if they have h publications that have each been cited at least h times.

For example, an h-index of 10 means the researcher has 10 papers that have each received at least 10 citations. This index balances quantity and quality, rewarding consistent and impactful scholarship rather than a single highly cited paper.

The h-index is widely used in promotion, tenure, and grant evaluations because it reflects sustained academic influence over time.

The i10-index Explained

The i10-index represents the number of publications that have received at least 10 citations. It is a simpler metric that highlights how many of an academic’s works have achieved a basic level of recognition within the scholarly community.

Although it is mainly used by Google Scholar, the i10-index is helpful for early-career researchers, as it provides a clear picture of growing research influence.

Why These Metrics Matter

Citation metrics are not about competition or self-promotion. They provide objective evidence of how research contributes to knowledge, policy, and practice. Understanding one’s h-index and i10-index helps academics identify research strengths, track growth, and plan future scholarly activities strategically.

More importantly, these metrics allow institutions to make informed decisions based on transparent and verifiable data.

Recommendation

All academic staff are strongly encouraged to create and regularly update their Google Scholar profiles. Publications should be correctly attributed, duplicates removed, and affiliations accurately stated.

Maintaining a Google Scholar profile is no longer optional in modern academia. It is a professional responsibility that benefits the individual scholar, the institution, and the wider research community.

By taking ownership of our academic visibility and impact, we position ourselves and our institutions for greater recognition, collaboration, and scholarly influence.

Human-Centered and Culturally Responsive AI in Education: Bridging Global Trends and African Realities

Guest Lecture: SAARMSTE Lesotho Chapter Annual Conference, November 2025   |   Format: Virtual Guest

On Saturday, November 8, Mr. Matthew Nyaaba had the privilege of serving as a Guest Speaker (virtually) at the Annual Conference of the Southern African Association for Research in Mathematics, Science, and Technology Education (SAARMSTE), Lesotho Chapter, through the kind courtesy of Mr. Sechaba Koma. Mr. Nyaaba began his presentation with a captivating AI-generated video podcast, created using ChatGPT and NotebookLM, which introduced his professional and research background. This innovative opening immediately captured participants’ attention and exemplified the creative ways AI can be used for academic self-presentation.

He opened the session by discussing the global diffusion of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and invited participants to share their own experiences using AI tools in education and research. Participants mentioned using ChatGPT, Copilot, and Canva for writing, design, and professional tasks. This discussion transitioned into an analysis of OpenAI’s Global Usage Insights (Mid-2025) and Microsoft’s AI Diffusion Report (2025), two reports that provided global perspectives on AI adoption and use.

Findings from the OpenAI Global Usage Report (Mid-2025)

Drawing from the OpenAI Usage Report, Mr. Nyaaba explained that AI use has become increasingly inclusive, with the gender gap narrowing from 37% in January 2024 to 52% female-identified users by July 2025. He noted that the fastest growth occurred in low- and middle-income countries, where engagement increased more than fourfold by May 2025.

He further outlined key usage patterns from the report:

  • Usage intents: Asking (49%), Doing (40%), and Expressing (11%).
  • Writing remains the dominant work-related task.
  • Approximately 70% of AI use is non-work related, compared to 30% for work purposes.

These statistics resonated strongly with participants’ responses during the session. As they described how they engage with AI tools, their practices reflected the same global patterns identified by OpenAI—asking questions, performing practical or academic tasks, and using AI for creative expression. This connection between global data and participants’ lived experiences reinforced the universality of AI engagement across diverse cultural and economic contexts.

Insights from the Microsoft AI Diffusion Report (2025)

Mr. Nyaaba also highlighted findings from the Microsoft AI Economy Institute’s AI Diffusion Report (2025), one of the first comprehensive attempts to measure global AI adoption rates. The report shows significant disparities in diffusion across regions. Countries such as Singapore (58.6%), UAE (59.4%), and Norway (45.3%) lead in AI adoption, while most of Africa (including Lesotho) remains below 10% diffusion.

He used this data to emphasize the urgent need for inclusive AI literacy across the African continent. With such limited diffusion, Africa’s representation in AI-generated content remains low, constraining the visibility of African knowledge systems, languages, and perspectives within global AI models. He noted that mitigating bias requires action at two levels: the developer level, through inclusive dataset design, and the educator level, through human-centered prompt engineering and classroom integration practices that emphasize equity and contextual relevance.

Engaging Dialogue and Ethical Reflections

The session was highly interactive. One participant, Dr. Lucia Lisene, raised a question about how to assess students in the age of AI, particularly how teachers can design questions that discourage academic misuse. Mr. Nyaaba commended the concern and explained that AI should be seen as a supportive learning partner and emphasis should be on context over content. He encouraged educators to refocus education toward performance-based and critical thinking assessments, moving beyond traditional recall-based evaluations that often enable academic dishonesty.

He further encouraged participants to integrate AI intentionally into their course outlines, establish classroom-level AI guidelines, and experiment with prompt-based assessments, a strategy where students critically evaluate AI-generated responses as part of their learning process. Another participant (Mr. Lereko Mohafa) asked about the ethical use of AI in research, particularly regarding data analysis and plagiarism risks. Mr. Nyaaba acknowledged that AI-assisted data analysis is becoming increasingly common but cautioned researchers to uphold ethical standards. He pointed out that tools such as ChatGPT now offer data privacy controls, allowing users to disable data sharing, but advised avoiding the use of sensitive personal or institutional information.

Conclusion and Reflections

The session concluded with participants expressing deep appreciation for the opportunity to learn, reflect, and exchange ideas on the human-centered and responsible use of AI in education. The Lesotho Chapter, led by Dr. Sikeme Raphoka… commended Mr. Nyaaba’s presentation for bridging global data insights with African educational realities. Through this dialogue, Mr. Nyaaba underscored that Africa must move from being AI consumers to active co-creators, by fostering critical AI literacy, developing local content, and ensuring that African perspectives are represented in the evolving landscape of global AI innovation.

Guiding Students in the Age of Artificial Intelligence

Artificial Intelligence has rapidly become an integral part of the modern knowledge ecosystem. In education, its presence is no longer speculative or experimental — it is a reality students encounter daily.

Through digital tools, intelligent search systems, automated writing assistants, and adaptive learning platforms, AI is woven into the academic experience. The critical question for educators and institutions is therefore not whether students will use Artificial Intelligence, but how they should use it.

Beyond Prohibition — Toward Guided Engagement

A growing number of institutions have responded to generative AI by attempting to restrict or prohibit its use in academic work. While these concerns often arise from legitimate fears about academic dishonesty or overdependence on automated systems, outright prohibition may not be the most productive response.

Instead, the more responsible and forward-looking approach is to provide structured oversight and guidance. Students who engage with Artificial Intelligence under thoughtful academic supervision are better positioned to develop the competencies required for the digital age than those who are completely denied access to these technologies.

AI as a Cognitive Support Tool

Artificial Intelligence, when used responsibly, can support deeper learning. It can assist students in exploring complex concepts, generating ideas for research, organizing information, and engaging with diverse perspectives. Rather than replacing intellectual effort, it can function as a cognitive support tool that stimulates inquiry and critical reflection.

However, this potential can only be realized when students are guided to use AI responsibly. Without guidance, students may misuse these tools, rely on them uncritically, or fail to develop the analytical judgment required to evaluate AI-generated outputs. This is why structured oversight is essential.

What Oversight Really Means

Oversight does not mean control in the restrictive sense. Rather, it involves creating clear academic expectations and teaching students how to interact with AI tools in an ethical, reflective, and intellectually productive manner. Educators must help students understand when AI can support learning and when independent reasoning must take precedence.

For example, students can be taught how to use AI for:

  • Brainstorming and refining research ideas
  • Summarizing large bodies of literature
  • Generating alternative perspectives on a topic
  • Improving academic writing

At the same time, they should be trained to critically evaluate AI outputs, verify information from credible sources, and acknowledge the role of AI tools when appropriate.

Building Essential Digital Competencies

In this sense, Artificial Intelligence becomes part of the learning environment rather than an external threat to it. When students engage with AI critically and responsibly, they develop essential digital competencies — technological literacy, information evaluation, and adaptive thinking.

These competencies are increasingly necessary in a world where AI systems are becoming embedded in professional practice across fields such as education, engineering, business, healthcare, and the creative industries. Preparing students for this reality is therefore an educational responsibility.

A Shift in Educational Philosophy

Institutions that provide structured AI guidance are helping students develop not only technical familiarity with emerging tools but also the intellectual discipline required to use them wisely. Students who learn under such oversight are more likely to become critical thinkers, technologically competent professionals, and cognitively adaptive learners.

This highlights an important shift in educational philosophy. Instead of framing Artificial Intelligence as a disruptive force that must be resisted, it should be approached as a transformative tool that must be understood, guided, and integrated responsibly into academic practice.

The Task Ahead for Educators

For educators, the task ahead is clear. Rather than focusing solely on restricting student use of AI, institutions should invest in developing:

  • Clear and transparent AI use policies
  • Training programmes for staff and students
  • Pedagogical strategies that promote responsible AI engagement

The goal is not to replace human thinking with machine assistance. The goal is to cultivate learners who can work intelligently with advanced technologies while maintaining intellectual integrity and critical judgment.

"Students who learn to navigate Artificial Intelligence under thoughtful academic oversight will be better prepared to thrive in the knowledge societies of the future. They will not only understand how AI works — but also how to question it, evaluate it, and use it as a tool for meaningful learning and innovation."

In this digital era, guided engagement with Artificial Intelligence is not simply an option for education. It is a necessity.
